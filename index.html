Used Coffee Grounds
Don't Have to Be Waste

I'm collecting used coffee grounds from the office kitchen. Two reasons: they feed my worm farm, and they go straight into my compost — where they turn into free fertiliser for plants.

Where to drop them off

Why I'm doing this

1. Feeding the worms

Worms love coffee grounds. They're soft, high in nitrogen, and the filter paper is food for them too. In a worm farm the grounds get eaten and turned into vermicompost — a rich, gentle fertiliser that's gentler on plant roots than most commercial products.

2. Composting

Grounds go straight into my compost bin, where they help balance the mix. Composting breaks them down into nutrients that plants can actually absorb, and returns them to the soil instead of the bin.

3. Cutting waste

Coffee grounds are heavy wet waste. In our old office in Kent St we had a dedicated organic bin, so grounds had a proper home. In the new office that bin doesn't exist — which means grounds now end up in the normal red bin, where they just rot alongside everything else.

That's a waste in every sense. Collecting them keeps a heavy wet stream out of general rubbish, means less to be collected, less methane from landfill, and fewer nutrients thrown away. This bin is simply bringing back what we had at Kent St. Small habit, real effect.

What it becomes: fertiliser

Fresh coffee grounds are quite acidic and can be salty, so they are not plant food on their own. Once they're composted — with leaves, cardboard, and time — the acidity balances out and the nutrients turn into something gentle and useful.

Nitrogen (N)

Feeds leafy growth. Greens stay green. Ends up helping the compost pile heat up as it breaks down.

Phosphorus (P)

Supports roots, flowers and fruit. Good for seedlings and fruiting plants once composted.

Potassium (K)

Helps plants fight disease and use water efficiently. One of the main benefits of finished compost.

Organic matter

Improves soil structure and water retention. Sandy soil holds more water, clay soil drains better.

In short: grounds in → compost out → better soil → healthier plants. Nothing wasted, everything returned to the garden.

Questions you might be asking

Can I just pour coffee grounds straight onto my plants?

No. Fresh grounds are quite acidic and can burn plant roots, especially on tender plants and seedlings. Think of it like concentrated fertiliser — too much is harmful. If you want to use them raw, mix them well into your soil first, keep the amount modest, and stop if the leaves start to yellow or curl.

So are they just useless then?

Not at all. They're excellent as a compost ingredient, and the finished compost is one of the best things you can put on your plants. The problem is only using them raw and unblended.

Do worms actually eat them?

Yes, they love them. Grounds are soft and nitrogen-rich, and the paper filters get shredded and eaten too. One note: add them in moderation and bury them in the bedding, because a big sudden pile of fresh grounds can heat up and overload the worms. Mix them in gradually.

What about the plastic cup lids, sleeves and stirrers?

Those don't compost in a home bin or worm farm. Please keep them out and put them in the recycling or general waste — the goal is collecting grounds and paper filters only.

Do I need to separate the grounds from the filter?

No need — paper filters are fine and welcome. Just pull off any plastic lid or pod capsule if there is one. If it's a coffee pod, check the type: some are compostable, most plastic ones are not.

What if there is milk or sugar left in the cup?

A little is fine. A big amount of dairy or added sugar can attract pests and make things go soggy, so please scrape out the bulk of it and give the grounds a light rinse if they're wet and messy.

How long does it all take?

A warm compost bin can turn soft grounds into usable compost in roughly 6–12 weeks with regular turning. A worm farm gives you vermicompost in about 2–3 months. Both need a bit of attention — that's the whole point.

Is this actually helping anything?

Yes, at every level. It diverts waste from landfill, returns nutrients to soil instead of burning them, and grows food for the worms. And if you take a handful home now and then, you've got a worm farm and a compost bin that keep producing for months.
