Why I'm doing this

Feeding the worms

Worms love coffee grounds — they're soft, nitrogen-rich, and the paper filters are food for them too. The result is vermicompost: a rich, gentle fertiliser that's far kinder on plant roots than most commercial products.

Cutting waste

In our old office at Kent St we had a dedicated organic bin, so grounds had a proper home. In the new office that bin doesn't exist, so they now end up in the normal red bin and just rot there.

Questions you might be asking

Can I just pour coffee grounds straight onto my plants?

No. Fresh grounds are acidic and can burn plant roots, especially on seedlings. They're like concentrated fertiliser — too much does harm. Compost them first, then use the finished compost on your plants.

What about plastic lids, pods and stirrers?

Those don't compost at home. Please keep them out — recycling or general waste. Grounds and paper filters only. Not every pod is compostable either, so check the type.

What happens if there's milk or sugar left in the cup?

A little is fine, but a lot of dairy or sugar attracts pests and makes things soggy. Scrape out the bulk and give wet, messy grounds a light rinse.

What kind of worms are you using?

Red wigglers — the standard choice for a home worm farm. You can buy them from Bunnings, and many councils offer a subsidy through compostrevolution.com.au, which has starter kits at a discount. Worth checking before you buy.

I have vermiphobia. How can I still start?

Skip the worms entirely — compost the grounds directly.

Collect the grounds, put them in a container with a lid, somewhere with no direct sunlight. Wait for mould to grow over them. Once they no longer smell like coffee, they're ready to use. In summer this takes about 1.5–2 months, in winter maybe 2–3 months.
